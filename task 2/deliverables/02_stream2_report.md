## 0. What was enforced before any number was computed

The complaint file has 2,219,844 problem rows describing 2,196,887 distinct complaints from July 1, 2017 to October 14, 2026. Filtering `problem_code` to `NO HEAT` and `NO HEAT AND NO HOT WATER` keeps 1,853,073 rows, and dropping the six Christmas-week logging-gap dates removes 79 more, leaving 1,852,994 heat rows that describe 1,843,856 distinct heat complaints at 107,825 building ids. Every count in this report is a count of distinct `complaint_id` per `building_id`, never a count of rows, because 45.1% of heat rows carry `problem_duplicate_flag = Y` and 49.7% carry `complaint_anonymous_flag = Y`. In the 2025-26 heat season the heat-only distinct complaint count is 303,353, which sits below the 344,437 figure the agency publishes because the published figure also counts hot-water complaints.

`building_id`, `bbl` and `bin` were cast to 64-bit integers after stripping decimal strings, and all Housing Preservation and Development tables were joined to each other on `building_id` only. The 116,139 building ids that appear in heat complaints or heat violations were given one `bbl` each (from the complaint rows themselves for 115,912 of them and from Active HPD Buildings or registration records for 227 more), and 111,657 of them matched a tax lot in the full Primary Land Use Tax Lot Output file on `bbl`. Of the 107,825 complaining buildings, 36,006 (33.4%) sit on lots with one or two residential units and would have been invisible in the 172,485-lot three-plus-unit universe, and 19.5% sit on lots that hold more than one building, which is why nothing in this report joins two agency tables on `bbl`. The full-lot file pulled on the server reproduces the team's `pluto_res3plus.csv` exactly (172,485 of 172,485 three-plus-unit lots overlap). The violation table lost 23,607 rows with inspection dates before January 1, 2010 (the earliest raw date is December 6, 1968), keeping 199,823 rows of which 68,739 are heat violations (orders 666, 966 and 674, Administrative Code section 27-2029) and 131,084 are hot-water violations (orders 670 and 970, section 27-2031). The last inspection date in the file is October 14, 2026, so every 2025-26 cluster has a complete seven-day and fourteen-day follow-up window. The `currentstatus` and `problem_status_date` columns were never loaded. Our daily minimum temperature matches Emerson's `days.csv` on all 3,385 shared days.

Seasons run October 1 to May 31 and are named by their starting year, so 2025-26 is the holdout. Training features use the seven seasons 2018-19 through 2024-25 and nothing dated on or after October 1, 2025.

## 1. Label credibility and violation confirmation (Deliverable 1)

Stream 5's label is two or more distinct heat complaints at the same building within 48 hours. To test what that label means, every heat complaint was assigned to a cluster at its building: a complaint starts a new cluster when it arrives more than 48 hours after the first complaint of the current cluster, which yields 1,031,133 clusters, of which 334,361 (32.4%) contain two or more distinct complaints and together hold 62.2% of all heat complaints. The median two-plus cluster spans 24.0 hours from its first to its last complaint. Each cluster was then matched to the first heat violation inspected at the same `building_id` on or after the cluster start date, with a seven-day primary window and a fourteen-day sensitivity window. The table below covers the 1,027,559 clusters that started on a heat-season day between 2017-18 and 2025-26.

| cluster tier | clusters | confirmed by a heat violation within 7 days | within 14 days | median lag when confirmed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| exactly 1 complaint | 693,823 | 4.78% | 6.22% | 3 days |
| 2 or more complaints (the primary label) | 333,736 | 8.93% | 11.49% | 3 days |
| 2 or more from 2 or more distinct apartments | 198,705 | 10.54% | 13.34% | 3 days |
| 2 or more non-duplicate complaints | 61,568 | 10.84% | 13.34% | 3 days |
| 3 or more complaints | 151,674 | 10.61% | 13.53% | 3 days |
| 5 or more complaints | 51,865 | 12.31% | 15.42% | 3 days |

Moving from one isolated complaint to the two-plus label multiplies the seven-day confirmation rate by 1.87 (4.78% to 8.93%), and the three-plus tier multiplies it by 2.22. The multiplier is stable across windows: 1.88 on the training seasons (4.65% to 8.73%) and 1.83 on the 2025-26 holdout (6.37% to 11.62%). Requiring the two complaints to come from two distinct pseudonymized apartments lifts confirmation to 10.54% and removes the single-tenant pattern seen at Building 68482 on East 217 Street, where 96% of 6,760 complaints were duplicates from one apartment. Counted on Stream 5's calendar-day grid instead of on clusters, there are 590,941 positive building-days (429,240 in the training seasons and 100,002 in 2025-26), 9.16% of them are followed by a heat violation within seven days (12.09% in 2025-26), and every one of them sits inside a two-plus cluster, so the two definitions agree.

Confirmation also depends on the weather on the day the cluster starts. For two-plus clusters across all seasons it is 10.4% on Frigid days (minimum below 20 F), 10.8% on Freezing days (20 to 31 F), 8.9% on Cold days (32 to 44 F) and 3.8% on Mild days (45 F and above); in 2025-26 the same four numbers are 14.6%, 13.3%, 11.4% and 3.6%. Widening Frigid to below 25 F changes the picture very little. Building size matters more than era: two-plus clusters at one-to-two-unit homes are confirmed 14.4% of the time and at 3-to-29-unit buildings 12.9%, against 6.8% for 30-to-99-unit buildings and 5.9% for buildings with 100 or more units, because a large building generates many clusters that one inspection resolves. Pre-1901 buildings confirm at 15.3%, pre-war 1901-1939 buildings at 9.3%, 1940-1969 buildings at 6.1%, 1970-1999 buildings at 8.4% and post-2000 buildings at 10.7%, and buildings with a recorded alteration confirm slightly more often than unaltered ones (9.5% against 8.6%). The full breakdown, including the training and holdout windows separately, is in `violation_confirmation_summary.csv`.

### The inspector saturation proof

`violation_saturation_by_day.csv` holds one row per heat-season day with the complaint count, the duplicate-row share, the number of two-plus clusters started, their seven-day confirmation, the median inspection lag and the number of heat violations inspected in the following seven days. In 2025-26 the fourteen-day sub-freezing stretch from January 23 to February 5, 2026 produced 50,968 heat complaints and 9,583 two-plus clusters, 15.9% of which were confirmed within seven days, with 57.4% of problem rows flagged as duplicates. The February 7 to 9 snap, with Central Park lows of 6, 3 and 10 F, produced 13,222 heat-only complaints from 6,382 buildings, 2,366 two-plus clusters and 2,281 failing buildings; 11.8% of those clusters were confirmed within seven days and 55.1% of the rows were duplicates. February 8, 2026 is the single busiest day in the file at 5,751 heat-only complaints (6,144 when hot water is included), 958 two-plus clusters started that day and only 9.8% of them were confirmed within seven days, the lowest value of any sub-freezing day in the season with more than 500 clusters started (January 31 is next at 11.0% and January 30 at 12.1%).

Averaged across the six 2025-26 days with 4,000 or more complaints, confirmation of two-plus clusters is 13.1% against 13.4% on the 35 moderate sub-freezing days with 800 to 2,000 complaints, so the per-cluster rate barely moves while the fixed inspector pool gets much thinner: the median lag from cluster start to inspection grows from 3 to 4 days, the duplicate share rises from 50.2% to 57.6%, and the number of heat violations inspected in the following seven days per 100 complaints received falls from 36.8 to 25.1, a drop of one third. Across all nine seasons, restricted to Frigid and Freezing days so the temperature is held roughly constant, the confirmation of two-plus clusters rises with volume from 7.7% on days with 501 to 1,000 complaints to 11.6% on days with 3,001 to 4,500 complaints, then falls to 10.4% on the twelve days above 4,500 complaints, which have a mean low of 8 F. The honest reading for the video is that confirmation per cluster holds up until the pool is overwhelmed on the very worst days, and that the measurable saturation signal is the inspection yield per complaint and the extra day of lag, not a collapse in the confirmation rate. `fig_violation_confirmation.png` shows the tier bars, the band comparison and the per-day scatter for 2025-26 with February 7, 8 and 9 labeled.

## 3. The Forecast-Only building group (Deliverable 3)

Using only the seven training seasons, every building was scored on how many heat-season days it had at least one heat complaint in each weather band. The training window holds 1,698 heat-season days: 50 Frigid (below 20 F; 131 below 25 F), 305 Freezing, 669 Cold and 674 Mild. A building is Chronic if it complained on at least one Mild day (minimum 45 F or warmer), Forecast-Only strict if it never complained on a Mild or a Cold day but did on a Freezing or Frigid day, and Forecast-Only broad if it never complained on a Mild day but did on a sub-freezing day (which adds the buildings that also reacted on Cold days). A fourth group, Cold-only, complained only on 32 to 44 F days.

| group (training window 2018-19 to 2024-25) | buildings | residential units | share that failed (two-plus cluster) in 2025-26 | share that failed Feb 7 to 9, 2026 | buildings failed Feb 7 to 9 | complaint-days per 1,000 buildings per day in 2025-26, Frigid vs Mild |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chronic | 38,549 | 2,976,945 | 27.2% | 4.65% | 1,794 | 46.1 vs 7.2 (6.4 times) |
| Forecast-Only strict | 18,814 | 491,865 | 3.4% | 0.38% | 71 | 5.1 vs 0.45 (11.2 times) |
| Forecast-Only broad, the additional buildings | 14,991 | 530,520 | 12.0% | 1.53% | 229 | 17.8 vs 1.5 |
| Cold-only | 17,481 | 301,303 | 2.5% | 0.24% | 43 | 3.2 vs 0.4 |

The Forecast-Only strict group is 18,814 buildings holding 491,865 apartments, 10,784 of which reacted on a day below 25 F, and 9,729 of which are in the three-plus-unit model universe (40.6% are one-to-two-unit homes). Their profile is a smaller and slightly younger version of the Chronic group: median 3 apartments against 12, median year built 1930 against 1928, 52.5% pre-war against 64.0%, 15.1% in the Bronx against 23.1%, and 72.8% of them had zero complaints in 2024-25, so a list ranked by last season's complaints cannot see them at all. The broad group adds 14,991 buildings with 530,520 apartments, for a total of 33,805 zero-mild-weather buildings holding 1,022,385 apartments. Out of sample the regime holds: in the 2025-26 winter the strict group complained at 5.1 complaint-days per 1,000 buildings per day on the 19 Frigid days and 0.45 on the 92 Mild days, an eleven-fold swing against the Chronic group's 6.4-fold swing. Scored against the archived forecasts rather than the observed temperature, the strict group's rate on the 119 days when the previous morning's 48-hour forecast minimum was below 32 F was 2.12 per 1,000 per day, against 0.62 on the other 124 days.

Intensity matters within the group. Among the 33,805 broad zero-mild buildings, those with a single sub-freezing complaint-day in seven seasons failed in 2025-26 at 3.3%, those with two or three at 7.8%, those with four to nine (3,705 buildings, 173,250 apartments) at 19.2%, and the 814 buildings with ten or more sub-freezing complaint-days and still zero mild-weather complaints (50,273 apartments) failed at 44.0% in 2025-26 and 7.5% in the three-day snap. The 4,519 broad buildings with four or more sub-freezing complaint-days hold 223,523 apartments and failed at 23.7% in 2025-26.

### February 7 to 9, 2026 out of sample

In the snap 2,281 buildings holding 229,057 apartments crossed the two-plus threshold. Of these, 1,794 were Chronic, 300 were zero-mild buildings from the broad Forecast-Only group (71 strict) holding 26,529 apartments, 43 were Cold-only, and 145 had no training complaints at all. 412 of the 2,281 failing buildings (18.1%) had zero complaints in 2024-25. Ranking all 116,139 buildings by last season's complaint total and inspecting the top k gives the static baseline; the table reports the share of the top k that failed in the snap.

| inspection budget k | static: last season's total | static: seven-season total | last season's heat violations | recency: trailing 14 days before Feb 7 | cold-sensitivity: sub-freezing reaction rate, training window |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 56% | 55% | 30% | 72% | 59% |
| 500 | 41% | 35% | 24% | 57% | 41% |
| 1,000 | 35% | 31% | 22% | 48% | 34% |
| 2,500 | 26% | 24% | 17% | 35% | 26% |

The cold-sensitivity ranking alone beats both static lists at k = 100 and ties the last-season list at larger k, and its value shows up in the new-to-list cut: of its top 100 buildings, 67 are absent from the static top 100 and 33 of those 67 (49%) failed in the snap; at k = 500 it adds 271 buildings the static list misses, 84 of which failed, and at k = 2,500 it adds 1,059, 162 of which failed. The strongest single ranking is recency, which Stream 5 also found, and its 72% at k = 100 is the number the model has to beat; the model-iterations document reports 67.7% precision at 100 averaged over all 71 cold test days against 61.7% for recency, which is consistent with what we see here on the three worst days. The Forecast-Only strict buildings that failed in the snap sit far down every list: their median rank on the last-season list is 32,925 (best 11,686) and none of them is inside the top 2,500 of any ranking, which is the honest statement of what the group is. It is a long tail of 18,814 low-frequency buildings, 71 of which failed in the snap, and the forecast trigger is what makes it worth looking at them at all, because on a mild day they produce nothing. The group's `baseline_rank_last_season` and `cold_sensitivity_rank` columns are in the handoff file so Stream 5 can measure its own model on exactly this cut.

### Sid's archived 48-hour forecasts versus what happened

The archived forecast issued at 12:00 UTC on each day was compared with the lowest Central Park reading over the following two calendar days for the 243 heat-season prediction days of 2025-26 covered by the file. The sub-freezing flag agrees on 86.0% of days, the forecast caught 98.9% of the 87 observed sub-freezing windows and raised a false alarm on 27.7% of its 119 sub-freezing calls, with a mean absolute error of 5.8 F and a cold bias of 4.8 F, which is expected because the forecast minimum is the coldest hour at a model grid point while the observation is a single station's daily low. The snap was forecast-triggered two days ahead: the citywide 48-hour minimum issued on the morning of February 5 was 6.1 F (observed 6 F), on February 6 it was minus 0.9 F (observed 3 F) and on February 7 it was minus 1.1 F (observed 3 F), with the Bronx grid point at minus 0.4 and minus 0.8 F. `forecast_vs_observed_2025_26.csv` holds the day-by-day comparison.

## 4. Boiler defects and fuel joined on BIN (Deliverable 4)

Both second-tier tables were pulled on the LinuxONE server with `curl` in `$limit=50000` pages ordered by `:id` (892,914 Department of Buildings boiler inspection reports from `52dp-yji6` in 18 pages and 91,370 Department of Environmental Protection boiler fuel records from `f4rp-2kvy` in two) and joined to the building universe on `bin` cast to int64. The Department of Buildings table covers 84,296 distinct BINs and the Department of Environmental Protection table 57,144. A Department of Buildings boiler record exists for 37.3% of the 116,139 buildings in the universe and for 54.7% of the three-plus-unit buildings, rising to 82.5% of 30-to-99-unit buildings, while only 10.6% of one-to-two-unit homes have one, since small homes are exempt from the annual boiler inspection. A Department of Environmental Protection fuel record exists for 25.1% of the universe and 37.4% of three-plus-unit buildings. Among buildings with a Department of Buildings record, 37.3% had at least one report with `defects_exist = Yes` before October 1, 2025.

Every feature uses only reports and registrations dated before October 1, 2025: the number of boiler reports and of defect reports, the defect share, the date and defect status of the last inspection, days since the last inspection at the cutoff, reports and defects in the last three years, late-filing fees, pressure type, boiler make, the primary and secondary fuel from the latest registration, a fuel grade (natural gas, No. 2, No. 4 or No. 6 oil), the year of the first registration as a boiler-age proxy, and whether the latest certificate had expired at the cutoff.

A prior logged boiler defect lifts the 2025-26 failure rate by 28% (31.9% against 24.9%), the verified-failure rate by 53% (7.0% against 4.6%) and the seven-day confirmation of each cluster from 9.9% to 11.9%; the lift on the snap itself is 5.4% against 4.1%. Over the whole universe the comparison is 29.4% against 22.8% failing and 6.4% against 4.2% verified, with buildings that have no boiler record at all failing at 5.5% because most of them are small homes. Fuel grade carries a signal too: among three-plus-unit buildings with a boiler record, those burning No. 6 oil failed at 45.8% and No. 4 oil at 43.9%, against 31.3% for natural gas and 29.4% for No. 2 oil, and buildings whose last boiler inspection was three or more years old failed less often (18.2%) but were confirmed more often when they did (16.8%), which fits buildings that have dropped out of the inspection cycle.

The Forecast-Only buildings are not disproportionately defect-logged, and the report says so rather than claiming otherwise. Among three-plus-unit buildings with a boiler record, 41.2% of Chronic buildings have a prior defect against 32.4% of Forecast-Only strict and 37.4% of the broad additions; mean years since first fuel registration are 47.5 for Chronic buildings against 36.6 for the strict group and 37.9 for the broad additions; and the oil-burning share among buildings with a fuel record is about 45% in every group. The strict group is younger and smaller mechanically, so the boiler tables confirm it is a different population from the chronic buildings rather than a hidden subset of them. `bin_boiler_features.csv` is keyed by `bin`, `building_id` and `bbl` with the leak-free columns listed above and four clearly named evaluation columns, ready for the Hour 7 merge into the Snap ML and XGBoost panel; `boiler_lift_summary.csv` and `boiler_profile_by_group.csv` hold every breakdown.

## 5. IBM z17 execution log (machine type 9175, s390x)

All numbers in this section were measured by `s2_00_stage_protect.py` on the real project files during the final `run_all.sh` pass on the guest (`processor 0: machine = 9175`, `uname -m` = s390x, kernel 6.8, three virtual cores; `/proc/cpuinfo` lists the `dflt`, `nnpa` and `vx` facilities; `lszcrypt` reports no Crypto Express card, so every cipher below runs on the on-core CPACF instructions). The hardware was switched off for the comparison rows with `DFLTCC=0` for the deflate engine and with `OPENSSL_s390xcap="km:0:0;kmc:0:0;kmctr:0:0;kmf:0:0;kmo:0:0;kma:0:0;kmac:0:0;kimd:0:0;klmd:0:0"` for the cipher and hash instructions, on the same process with the same bytes. The full table, including level 1 and the decompression rows, is `z17_hardware_log.md`.

| operation on the real file | hardware on | hardware off | speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| zlib level 6 compress, raw complaint table (344.8 MB) | 0.166 s, 2,077 MB/s, ratio 3.22 | 10.485 s, 33 MB/s, ratio 4.88 | 63 times |
| zlib level 6 compress, staged pseudonymized extract (266.6 MB) | 0.134 s, 1,997 MB/s | 7.988 s, 33 MB/s | 60 times |
| zlib level 6 decompress, raw complaint table | 0.510 s, 677 MB/s | about 1.26 s, 273 MB/s | 2.5 times |
| AES-256-GCM encrypt of the compressed extract (111.5 MB, OpenSSL through the cryptography package) | 0.146 s, 761 MB/s | 1.027 s, 109 MB/s | 7 times |
| AES-256-GCM encrypt of the raw table (344.8 MB, 64 MB chunks) | 0.473 s, 728 MB/s | 3.255 s, 106 MB/s | 7 times |
| SHA-256 digest of the raw table (344.8 MB) | 0.247 s, 1,396 MB/s | 1.326 s, 260 MB/s | 5.4 times |
| `openssl enc -aes-256-ctr -pbkdf2` on the staged extract (266.6 MB, command line) | 0.187 s, 1,428 MB/s | 1.508 s, 177 MB/s | 8 times |
| `openssl dgst -sha256 -hmac` on the staged extract (command line) | 0.122 s, 2,177 MB/s | 1.128 s, 236 MB/s | 9 times |
| `openssl speed -evp aes-256-gcm`, 16 KB blocks, one core | 14.17 GB/s | 0.13 GB/s | 109 times |
| `openssl speed -evp sha256`, 16 KB blocks, one core | 4.16 GB/s | 0.26 GB/s | 16 times |

The on-chip deflate engine compresses the 361 MB complaint table in a sixth of a second instead of ten and a half seconds, at the cost of a looser ratio (3.22 against 4.88), which is the right trade for extracts that are rewritten on every run. The cipher numbers through Python top out near 0.75 GB/s because a one-shot call on a 100 MB buffer is bounded by memory copies, while the same instructions reach 14 GB/s on cache-sized blocks; both are real, and the first is the one that applies to a file. During the staging pass the two shipped gzip streams were inflated on the server by the same engine, the 344.8 MB complaint table in 0.75 s (461 MB/s of output) and the 26.7 MB violation table in 0.02 s.

Apartment pseudonymization ran as HMAC-SHA256 over the string `building_id|apartment` with a 32-byte key that exists only in `work/` on the server: 2,219,844 complaint rows in 5.85 s (379,380 rows per second, limited by the Python call per row rather than by the hash), 223,430 violation rows in 0.63 s, turning 15,885 distinct raw apartment labels into 502,141 distinct 64-bit building-apartment tags, after which the raw column was dropped from every working table. Distinct complaining apartments per building and per cluster are still countable (they feed the 10.54% confirmation row in Section 1), and nothing below building level can be reconstructed from the bundle. The staged extracts sit on disk as `complaints_pseud.csv.zz` (DFLTCC deflate) and `complaints_pseud.csv.zz.gcm` (AES-256-GCM with a 12-byte nonce and the file label as associated data), and the decrypt-and-verify pass round-trips byte for byte.

For the video the sequence is `grep -o -w -E 'dflt|nnpa' /proc/cpuinfo`, then `./run_all.sh`, which prints the machine line and the timings above as it goes; the whole workstream, six scripts plus the notebook execution, completes in under ten minutes on the three-core guest (the six scripts in about five minutes, the notebook execution in 280 seconds, with no cell errors). The Telum II accelerator (`nnpa`) is present on the guest but was not used in this workstream; Stream 5's Snap ML measurements cover it.

Limitations to say out loud: the label is still tenants calling 311, a verified violation requires an inspector to have gone and found the condition, so confirmation rates measure the inspection system as much as the boilers; the Forecast-Only strict group is real but low-frequency, and its value comes from being ignored on mild days rather than from high precision on its own; the household-size multiplier of 2.55 is a citywide American Community Survey average applied uniformly; the Department of Buildings boiler table only covers buildings subject to annual inspection, so small homes are absent by design; and the archived forecast comparison mixes a grid-point minimum with a single station, which is why its error is larger than the 2 F Sid measured hour for hour.
