01 / Introduction

Why we built HeatAhead.

New York City law requires landlords to keep apartments at 68°F during the day and 62°F overnight from October 1 to May 31. Every winter, thousands of buildings fail to do so. When the heat goes out, tenants call 311, and the Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD) sends an inspector. The city largely works through these complaints in the order they arrive, so by the time an inspector reaches a building, the people inside may already have been cold for days. Since July 2017 alone, New Yorkers have filed more than 1.8 million heat complaints.

Using several open data sets and machine learning models, we set out to answer one primary question: on the evening before a freezing day, can a building’s complaint, violation and property history, combined with the weather, tell us which buildings are most likely to lose heat in the next few days? In particular, we wanted to know whether a ranked list built this way would put inspectors in front of failing buildings sooner than the approaches the city can use today, such as answering complaints in the order they arrive or looking at last winter’s complaint counts.

We expected a building’s own recent complaint history to be the strongest signal, because a heating system that has failed recently is likely to fail again. We also expected that how a building responds to cold weather, its size and age, the record of its owner’s other buildings and the condition of its boiler would each add information. This page explains the data we used, how we prepared it, and the methods we used to test those expectations. The website itself presents the results: each building’s estimated risk, the citywide map and the inspection priority list.

How to read a prediction

A high estimate is a reason to check a building early. It is not proof that the building’s heat is broken or that its owner has broken the law.

02 / The data

Where our data comes from and how we prepared it.

The data for this analysis describes New York City’s residential buildings, the heat complaints their tenants filed, the violations that inspectors recorded, the owners and boilers behind each building, and the weather on every day of the heating season. Most of it comes from NYC Open Data ↗, the city’s public data portal, where HPD and other city agencies publish their records. The rest comes from the Department of City Planning and from NOAA, the federal weather agency. All of it is public and free to access.

The table below lists each source, the agency that publishes it and what we took from it. Each name links to the original data set.

How the sources fit together

Because these records come from four city agencies and one federal agency, they do not share a single identifier. Complaints, violations and PLUTO identify a property by its BBL, the ten digit borough, block and lot number of the tax lot. Boiler records identify a building by its BIN, the Department of Buildings’ building number, and owner registrations use HPD’s own building ID. We joined each source only on the identifier it was published with, and then summarized everything at the level of the BBL.

Cleaning the complaint records

The complaint file required the most preparation. Its 2,219,844 rows describe 2,196,887 distinct complaints, because one complaint can list several problems, each on its own row. We kept the two problem types that describe a heating failure, “NO HEAT” and “NO HEAT AND NO HOT WATER,” which left 1,853,073 rows. We then removed 6,650 rows that had no BBL and could not be placed on a property, and kept only complaints at lots in our building table, which left 1,703,315 rows describing 1,694,922 distinct complaints at 62,451 lots. Finally, we counted each complaint once per building per day, so that a complaint repeated across rows never counts twice. The result is 1,148,420 building days with at least one heat complaint.

For the violation records, we kept only the five heat and hot water order numbers and removed 23,607 records dated before 2010, which include inspection dates as early as 1968 that are either entry errors or too old to describe a building today. For the boiler records, we count only reports dated before the winter being studied, so that a later inspection can never inform an earlier prediction. The weather record needed no repair.

These steps mean our study covers buildings with three or more apartments that have some heat complaint or violation history. A building enters a ranking only if it has at least one heat complaint or violation on record, because a building with no history gives a model nothing to learn from, and no method we tested could find the failures in those buildings. The winter outlook, which estimates each building’s chance of losing heat at least once this winter, covers {{ '{:,}'.format(about_snapshot.get('ranked', 63727)) }} buildings. The cold-day ranking, which estimates the chance of a heat failure within 48 hours of a freezing day, covers {{ '{:,}'.format(about_snapshot.get('cold_ranked', 67125)) }} buildings. The cold-day ranking is slightly larger because it also counts buildings whose only record is a hot water violation, while the winter outlook counts heat violations only.

Note, a BBL describes a tax lot rather than a single building, so a large complex with several buildings on one lot appears in our data as a single record.

The outcome we predict

Public records do not say when a boiler stops working, so we had to define a heat failure from what is recorded. We say a building has a heat failure when it receives two or more distinct heat complaints across two consecutive days. We require two complaints rather than one because a single complaint often describes one cold apartment or a call that leads nowhere. We checked this choice against inspectors’ records: a single isolated complaint was followed by a confirmed heat violation within seven days 4.78% of the time, while a cluster of two or more was followed by one 8.93% of the time, 1.87 times as often. Confirmed violations are rare even for real failures because inspectors cannot reach every complaint in time, so we train on the complaint based outcome and keep the confirmed violation, within seven days, as a separate check.

Privacy and ethics

The complaint and violation files include apartment numbers. Our modeling pipeline never loads that column, and our separate complaint analysis replaced every apartment number with a secure one way code before building any table, so no apartment number appears in any table, model input or page on this site. Every estimate describes a whole building. We also recognize that complaints depend on people choosing to report a problem, and that some communities report less often than others. A building with a quiet record is not necessarily a building without problems, and we discuss what that means for the results in the limitations section below.

03 / Modeling

How we built and trained the models.

Our modeling uses supervised machine learning. This section explains how we turned the cleaned records into model inputs, how we built the training data, which methods we used and why, and how we tested them.

Turning records into model inputs

Each training example is one building on one heating season day, described by 36 inputs calculated from the records above. The inputs fall into seven families. Previous winters count the building’s complaints and violations over the last one and two seasons, flag buildings with five or more complaints in each of the last two seasons, and count last season’s whole building complaints, which usually mean the boiler itself failed rather than one apartment. Recent activity counts the building’s complaints in the previous 2, 7, 14 and 30 days and measures how many days have passed since its last complaint and last violation. Cold response measures, over the previous two winters, the share of frigid days (below 20°F), freezing days (20°F to below 32°F) and cold days (32°F to below 45°F) on which the building had a complaint. This separates a building with constant problems from one that mainly struggles when the temperature drops. Property inputs describe the building itself using PLUTO: its number of residential units, floors, floor area, year built, whether it has been altered, and its community district, which lets the model compare buildings of similar size and age. Owner and boiler inputs count how many buildings the owner holds and how many complaints the owner’s other buildings received last winter, along with the boiler’s recorded defects and whether it burns oil. Nearby reports count complaints at other buildings on the same tax block in the previous two days, because buildings that share an owner or a heating plant tend to fail together. Finally, weather and calendar inputs describe the day’s temperature, snow and wind and how far into the cold season the day falls.

Building the training data

Using every building on every day would produce tens of millions of examples, nearly all of them days without a failure. Instead, we kept every heat failure from the 2018 to 2019 winter through the 2024 to 2025 winter and added twice as many randomly chosen days without one. After removing buildings with no prior history, the training set contains 622,362 building days. Because this sample contains far more failures than real life does, the model’s raw scores overstate the true chance of a failure, which is why every probability we show passes through a separate calibration step, described below.

The machine learning methods we use

Our main models are gradient boosted decision trees. A decision tree sorts buildings into groups by asking a series of yes or no questions about their inputs, such as whether a building had more than three complaints in the last 14 days. Gradient boosting builds hundreds of small trees one after another, with each new tree correcting the errors left by the trees before it. We chose this family because it is the most consistently accurate method for tables of counts, dates and measurements like ours, it handles missing values and very different scales without extra preparation, and it lets us explain which inputs a model relies on.

The original model averages two implementations of the same idea, XGBoost and scikit-learn’s HistGradientBoosting, each built from 400 trees with a maximum depth of 6 and a learning rate of 0.08. Averaging two models smooths out the quirks of either one. Its scores then pass through isotonic regression, a calibration method that learns how raw scores map to the share of buildings that actually failed, using every eligible building rather than the sampled training data. Calibration changes the numbers we display but never the order of the ranking.

We built two more models for the two other questions the site answers. The winter outlook uses one example per building per season, with 21 inputs measured on October 1 and the outcome “lost heat at least once that winter.” It is a HistGradientBoosting model trained on 305,413 building seasons, with its own isotonic calibration. The cold spell model is trained directly on the task inspectors face: on the evening before a cold spell, which buildings will fail during it? We chose its settings with a twelve trial search scored only on the 2024 to 2025 winter, then trained it five times with different random seeds and averaged the five, which removes most of the variation between individual training runs.

We also tested methods that work differently, to make sure the gradient boosted trees were not simply the first idea we tried. These include a model trained to rank buildings directly, a graph neural network that learns from links between buildings that share an owner or a block, a survival model that predicts when a building will first fail, a model trained on confirmed violations instead of complaints, and a statistical method called conformal risk control that sets alert thresholds with a guaranteed maximum miss rate. Finally, we used backward feature elimination, which removes the least useful input one at a time across three model types, to learn which inputs actually carry the signal. The development record below lists what each of these changed.

How we test the models

We split the data by time, never at random, so that a model is always tested on a winter it has never seen. Models learn from the 2018 to 2019 through 2023 to 2024 winters, the 2024 to 2025 winter is used to choose between versions and settings, and the 2025 to 2026 winter is held out and scored once at the end. On each test day, the model ranks every eligible building in the city, and we measure precision at k: of the k buildings ranked highest, the share that actually had a heat failure. We use this measure because an inspection team can only visit a limited number of buildings, so what matters is whether the top of the list is right. We compare every model with simple rules the city could use without any model, such as ranking by complaints in the last 14 days. To check that the results were not one lucky winter, we repeated the test six times, each time training only on earlier winters and testing on the next one, and we measured uncertainty by resampling whole days and whole cold spells rather than individual buildings.

  3. Winter 2025 to 2026Test once on the held out winter.

04 / Evaluation

How useful is the top of the list?

An inspection team can only visit so many buildings. We measure precision at k: among the first k buildings in the ranking, how many later meet the complaint-cluster label? This is more useful for prioritization than a single “accuracy” number for every building.

What a ranked list could change for inspections

To see what ranking could mean in practice, we replayed 183,384 building-day complaint cases from the 2025 to 2026 winter, comparing first-come-first-served dispatch with a queue ordered by our final cold spell model. At an assumed capacity of 700 inspections a day and a 14-day case expiry, the simulation estimated 7.8% more confirmed violations and a mean wait of 2.03 days instead of 3.12 for complaint-cluster cases.

These are simulated results, not an observed city deployment or proof that outages were prevented. Prioritizing also left some lower-ranked cases unvisited: 5,546 complaint-cluster cases expired in that scenario. That trade-off matters alongside the average improvement.

How much of the whole problem does the final ranking find?

Precision measures how many picks were right. Recall and cumulative gain ask how many of all the complaint-cluster outcomes the ranking finds.

  • For our final cold spell model, the top 1% of the ranked list captures 25.7% of spell outcomes on average.
  • The top 10% captures 75.3%.
  • At 2,500 buildings, mean recall is 54.3% across the eight spells.

These results describe the final cold spell model over the eligible population. They are not calibration results for every building, and they do not measure outcomes in properties outside the ranking.

The clearest lesson

Recent and past complaints carry much of the ranking signal. The largest later gain came from matching the training target to the operational question: a problem during the whole cold spell, rather than only a daily 48-hour window.

How the alert thresholds were tested

The threshold experiment fixes a model trained on 2018–19 through 2022–23, then uses 102 freezing days across the next two winters to choose broad and strict alert levels. It checks those levels on the 71 freezing days of 2025–26.

The broad level missed 7.4% of daily complaint-cluster outcomes, but would alert about 13,107 buildings per cold day on average. The strict level missed 21.1% and would alert about 5,220. Coverage comes with a larger notification volume.

These results do not guarantee the same miss rate in a different future winter. The email worker uses the tested threshold file when it is present; otherwise it has a documented fallback. An alert threshold and a top-50 inspection queue serve different purposes.

06 / Reading the estimates

A good ranking and a useful percentage are different tests.

The top-100 results describe the quality of a list. A building’s percentage needs another check: calibration. We group similar predictions and compare them with the share of buildings that actually had complaint clusters. A group estimated near 60% should have an observed rate near 60%.

Winter risk

Estimates whether a building will have at least one complaint cluster over the season, using information available at its start. This is the estimate used to color the risk map.

Cold-day risk

Estimates the shorter 48-hour outcome around freezing weather, with recent complaints and weather included. It answers a different question, so its percentage can differ.

For season risk, we fit the calibration adjustment on half the 2025–26 building records and checked it on the other half. Daily calibration uses a sample from the full ranking population, because sampling training examples changes the apparent complaint rate. Calibration checks are separate from the ranking tests.

Why we show bands

Displayed building probabilities are capped at 99% and shown as approximate bands from 20% upward. These bands keep the display from suggesting too much precision. They are not a measured confidence interval for an individual building.

08 / Limits & next steps

What this work cannot tell us yet.

In addition to explaining our methods, it is important to discuss the limits of our data and of our analysis. The first and largest limit is that our outcome is built from complaints. A complaint depends on a tenant choosing to call 311, and some buildings and communities report problems less often than others. A quiet record therefore does not establish that a building is safe, and a model trained on complaints can only learn the failures that people reported. We use confirmed violations as a second check, but those are limited by how many inspectors are available.

Our coverage is also incomplete. We study buildings with three or more apartments, and we rank only buildings with some complaint or violation history, so a building that is missing from the map or not individually ranked should not be read as having zero risk. Some property facts are current snapshots rather than historical records: ownership comes from today’s registrations, so our tests on past winters inherit today’s owners. Because a BBL describes a tax lot, a large complex can appear as one record and rank highly partly because its many units generate many complaints.

There are limits to the analysis as well. Our tests use the weather that actually occurred as a stand in for a forecast. We measured archived 24 to 48 hour forecasts at about two degrees Fahrenheit of error, so live performance should be close, but it is not identical. Our final test covers a single held out winter with eight cold spells, which is a small sample, and a citywide result can hide differences between boroughs, building types and neighborhoods. We also built a model that estimates when a building will first fail, but its estimates were not reliable enough to show on this site.

Our next steps are to test the models on another winter, to audit how well they work across communities, and to try the tool with the people who would use it. Those checks matter before anyone uses these rankings to allocate public resources.
