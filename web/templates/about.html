01 / The problem

A chance to prepare before the next cold snap.

A complaint tells us someone has already had a problem. We wanted to see whether the pattern before a cold spell could help identify buildings that need attention next.

HeatAhead connects that research to a practical website: a building’s history and estimated risk, a citywide risk map, an inspection priority list, and a monitor for published complaints and historical replays. Residents can use the information to prepare; owners can plan checks; inspection teams can compare priorities when time is limited.

What we are trying to improve

Earlier attention to likely heat problems. A prediction is a reason to check a building, not proof that its heat is broken or that its owner has violated the law.

02 / The data

Several public records, one building history.

Our building table covers 172,485 residential property records. The published winter-risk file covers {{ '{:,}'.format(about_snapshot.get('ranked', 63727)) }} records with usable history; the cold-day file covers {{ '{:,}'.format(about_snapshot.get('cold_ranked', 67125)) }}. These are different prediction tasks, so their eligible populations differ.

We started with heat complaints, building records and daily weather. We joined records using NYC property identifiers, counted distinct complaint IDs, and organized the data by building, day and winter. A BBL identifies a tax lot, which can contain more than one building.

Step by step: cleaning the data and defining the label
  1. Keep heat-related complaints: “NO HEAT” and “NO HEAT AND NO HOT WATER.”
  2. Normalize dates and count distinct complaint IDs for each property and day. Repeated source rows do not become extra complaints.
  3. Attach property, ownership and boiler records using their appropriate identifiers. Treat BBLs as integers; use building IDs and BINs for the relevant building-level joins.
  4. Add the count on day d to the count on day d+1. A total of at least two creates a positive complaint-cluster label.
  5. Keep a separate confirmed-outcome check: a recorded heat violation within seven days. Do not use a later complaint status or closing date as a prediction input.

In the team’s historical confirmation analysis, isolated single complaints were followed by a recorded violation within seven days 4.78% of the time, compared with 8.93% for clusters of at least two: a 1.87× association. That supports studying clusters, while still treating them as reports rather than confirmed equipment failures.

How our weather analysis becomes a model input

We group days by their daily minimum temperature: frigid below 20°F; freezing from 20°F to below 32°F; cold from 32°F to below 45°F; and mild at 45°F or above.

For each building, we calculate how often it had a complaint on days in each band during the previous two winters. Days with no complaints still count in the denominator. That helps separate a building with frequent problems from one that mainly struggles when temperatures drop.

03 / Training

Learn from past winters. Test on a later one.

We train supervised machine-learning models: we give them historical building information and an outcome to learn. The main methods use gradient-boosted decision trees, which combine many small decision trees to find patterns in counts, building characteristics and weather.

  1. Define the outcome before training

    Our main label is two or more distinct heat complaints across two consecutive calendar days, called the “48-hour” label in our experiments. The spell model asks whether that complaint cluster occurs during a cold spell. This is a reported-problem measure; a separate model studies confirmed heat violations.

  2. Build a picture of what was already known

    Inputs include complaints in the previous 2, 7, 14 and 30 days; time since the last complaint; previous-winter complaints and violations; cold-weather reaction rates; property and boiler details; owner-portfolio history; and recent reports on the same block. Complaint windows stop before the prediction day.

  3. Fit the models and compare alternatives

    The original model averages XGBoost and scikit-learn gradient-boosting predictions. Its training set contains 622,362 building-days from the 2018–19 through 2024–25 seasons. We include complaint-cluster examples and sample days without clusters to keep training manageable.

  4. Test the ranking on a later winter

    The sampled training set includes positive examples and about twice as many sampled negative examples before the eligibility filters. That makes model fitting practical, but it changes the apparent failure rate.

    We evaluate on the full eligible ranking population, not just the sampled training rows. We compare the model with simple rules, including recent complaint counts and previous-season history. The original ranking test uses the 2025–26 winter, held out from fitting the prediction model.

More detail: training settings and the later spell model

The original M1 recipe averages two models: XGBoost and scikit-learn HistGradientBoosting, each with 400 boosting rounds, maximum depth 6 and learning rate 0.08. Gradient boosting fits trees in sequence, with each new tree working on errors left by the earlier ones.

A later XGBoost model learns the spell-level outcome directly. We select its settings using a separate validation winter, then average five fits with different random seeds to reduce variation between runs.

  1. 2018–19 → 2023–24Fit candidate models.
  2. 2024–25Choose settings; then refit using the earlier winters and this one.
  3. 2025–26Evaluate the selected recipe on eight cold spells.

For the final spell model, M13, we tried 12 configurations on the validation winter. The reported winner used depth 7, learning rate 0.03 and 61 trees. We refit five random seeds and averaged their scores. A coarse blend search with the tuned daily model, M12, selected the pure five-model average.

One caveat in the later experiment: the final blend comparison reused the validation winter after refitting. That comparison is less independent than the initial settings search.

We also run six tests that move forward through time: train on earlier winters, test on the next one. Automated checks cover history windows and columns that could reveal an outcome after it happened.

After evaluation, deployment models are refit with the latest completed winter included. Those refits are separate from the models used for the reported test results.

The follow-up daily run beats the 14-day baseline on 67 of 71 days at the top 100, with one tie and three losses. A paired sign-flip test using 100,000 draws gives p ≈ 0.00001. On the eight spell comparisons, the original model beats the baseline in all eight (exact one-sided p = 1/256). These describe this test set, not certainty about future winters.

How much of the whole problem does the final ranking find?

Precision measures how many picks were right. Recall and cumulative gain ask how many of all the complaint-cluster outcomes the ranking finds.

  • For M13, the top 1% of the ranked list captures 25.7% of spell outcomes on average.
  • The top 10% captures 75.3%. Random selection would capture about 10%.
  • At 2,500 buildings, mean recall is 54.3% across the eight spells.
  • Mean precision-recall AUC is 0.434, compared with a mean outcome rate of 3.1%. AUC summarizes the ranking over many possible list lengths.

These are M13 research results over the eligible population. They are not calibration results for every building, and they do not measure outcomes in properties outside the ranking.

What our inspection simulation suggests

We replayed 183,384 building-day complaint cases from 2025–26, comparing first-come-first-served dispatch with a model-prioritized queue. At an assumed capacity of 700 inspections a day and a 14-day case expiry, the simulation estimated 7.8% more confirmed violations and a mean wait of 2.03 days instead of 3.12 for complaint-cluster cases.

These are simulated results, not an observed city deployment or proof that outages were prevented. Prioritizing also left some lower-ranked cases unvisited: 5,546 complaint-cluster cases expired in that scenario. That trade-off matters alongside the average improvement.

The clearest lesson

Recent and past complaints carry much of the ranking signal. The largest later gain came from matching the training target to the operational question: a problem during the whole cold spell, rather than only a daily 48-hour window.

How the alert thresholds were tested

The threshold experiment fixes a model trained on 2018–19 through 2022–23, then uses 102 freezing days across the next two winters to choose broad and strict alert levels. It checks those levels on the 71 freezing days of 2025–26.

The broad level missed 7.4% of daily complaint-cluster outcomes, but would alert about 13,107 buildings per cold day on average. The strict level missed 21.1% and would alert about 5,220. Coverage comes with a larger notification volume.

These results do not guarantee the same miss rate in a different future winter. The email worker uses the tested threshold file when it is present; otherwise it has a documented fallback. An alert threshold and a top-50 inspection queue serve different purposes.

06 / Reading the estimates

A good ranking and a useful percentage are different tests.

The top-100 results describe the quality of a list. A building’s percentage needs another check: calibration. We group similar predictions and compare them with the share of buildings that actually had complaint clusters. A group estimated near 60% should have an observed rate near 60%.

Winter risk

Estimates whether a building will have at least one complaint cluster over the season, using information available at its start. This is the estimate used to color the risk map.

Cold-day risk

Estimates the shorter 48-hour outcome around freezing weather, with recent complaints and weather included. It answers a different question, so its percentage can differ.

For season risk, we fit the calibration adjustment on half the 2025–26 building records and checked it on the other half. Daily calibration uses a sample from the full ranking population, because sampling training examples changes the apparent complaint rate. Calibration checks are separate from the ranking tests.

Why we show bands

Displayed building probabilities are capped at 99% and shown as approximate bands from 20% upward. These bands keep the display from suggesting too much precision. They are not a measured confidence interval for an individual building.

After evaluation, we refit the deployment model, export calibrated building scores, and run the asset builder to produce the small public files the site reads. Model exchange uses portable JSON rather than moving Python pickles between laptop and mainframe.

  1. Prepare labels and features: 01_build_panel.py.
  2. Fit and evaluate: 02_train.py, 03_evaluate.py, 05_backtest.py and 07_spell_eval.py.
  3. Generate deployment and winter scores: 06_final_scores.py and 09_season_risk.py.
  4. Package the website’s public data: web/build_assets.py.

Heavy processing stays on LinuxONE. The public Vercel site serves precomputed building estimates and maps, while account and monitoring features connect to backend services. The replay lets us demonstrate how incoming complaints can update a building’s priority.

08 / The help assistant

How the models and the chat fit together.

The prediction training above uses structured records and boosted trees. The help assistant uses a different process. Our complaint extract contains categories and flags, not narrative text; we did not fine-tune a language model on complaint stories.

  1. Match common wording locally

    The browser searches our {{ guide_bootstrap['items']|length }} written help topics using keywords, related words and typo handling. This works without a language-model connection.

  2. Use an existing LLM for an unfamiliar question, if configured

    The optional server connection asks a pretrained model to classify the question into a known topic. It receives the current question and, when relevant, the previous topic ID; it does not receive the whole chat or account records.

  3. Offer choices when the meaning is unclear

    The assistant can show several “Did you mean…” options. A provider suggestion is offered for the visitor to choose, rather than being presented as a new factual answer.

  4. Show the written answer and its page links

    The selected topic loads an answer from the help library. Building risk still comes from the prediction pipeline; the LLM does not generate a building score or perform account actions.

The LLM response contract and fallback

The prompt asks for a topic identifier, not a free-form answer. Its JSON schema permits only known topic IDs or “unknown.” For example:

{ "topic": "inspector" }

Building IDs are removed before an optional provider call, and common private-input patterns are rejected. If the provider is unavailable or returns an invalid topic, the chat falls back to the local help library. The site does not store the conversation.

09 / Limits & next steps

What this work cannot tell us yet.

Complaints depend on people reporting.
Missing reports can hide real problems. A quiet history does not establish that a building is safe.
Coverage is incomplete.
Individual rankings focus on buildings with usable complaint or violation history. Missing map coverage or an unranked building should not be read as zero risk.
Some property facts are current snapshots.
Historical backtests can inherit today’s ownership records. Time-filtered boiler inputs were checked in a separate study. Time-limited complaint windows do not make every source fully historical.
Large complexes can dominate.
More units can generate more reports. A highly ranked BBL can represent a complex, so it should not be mistaken for one small building.
The backtest is not a live forecast trial.
The main backtests use observed weather as a forecast stand-in. Future forecast errors, older property records and changes in reporting can affect performance.
A citywide result can hide local gaps.
More testing by borough, building type and neighborhood is needed before using the ranking to allocate public resources.
A score does not tell us the exact failure date.
We tested a timing model, but its absolute estimates were not reliable enough to ship.

Our next steps are to evaluate on another winter, audit performance across communities, and test the tool with the people who would use it. Those checks matter before making claims about real-world inspection outcomes.
