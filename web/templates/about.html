About & methods

Heat should never
be a surprise.

Every winter, thousands of New York City buildings lose heat during cold snaps — and the city finds out one 311 call at a time. HeatAhead flips that order: it predicts which buildings are likely to lose heat before the cold arrives, so inspectors, tenants and owners can act early instead of react late.

01 / What it is

A ranked list, and what it buys the city.

1

Score every building

When a cold spell approaches, the model scores every ranked residential building in the city. On IBM LinuxONE that takes under a second — 481,356 buildings per second.

2

Rank for inspection

Of the first 100 buildings flagged at the start of a spell, about 90 go on to experience a whole-building heat failure during that spell (held-out winters; details below).

3

Measured impact

We replayed the real 2025-26 winter — 183,384 complaint-days — under the city's actual capacity. Same inspectors, dispatched by our ranking instead of first-come-first-served: 7.8% more violations confirmed, failing buildings reached in 2.0 days instead of 3.1, cold-household-days cut in half.

02 / Methods

Public data in, one honest probability out.

The model answers: “a cold spell is starting now — will this building have a whole-building heat failure at some point during the spell?” Every input is public data, dated strictly before the moment of prediction.

What it looks at

Heat-complaint history over several trailing windows · past-winter failures and verified violations · the building itself (age, size, units, boiler records) · the landlord's other buildings · complaints next door in the last 48 hours · the forecast.

How we keep it honest

The model only ever sees the past: features predate the prediction moment, and the entire 2025-26 winter stayed untouched until final evaluation. Training rolls forward across six winters, always testing on the next unseen one — it beat the strongest recency baseline in 6 of 6. A nine-test leakage suite runs against the codebase itself.

  • Headline precision at the top 100: 69.1% at 48 hours, 86.1% per cold spell, 96.6% at 30 days, 99% for the season. Our newest spell-level model reaches 90.1% — evaluated on held-out data it was allowed to see exactly once.
  • Alternatives we built and rejected on evidence: a learning-to-rank model and a graph neural network, both beaten by the simpler model under identical tests.

03 / Why “top 100”?

Why we grade the top of a list, not “accuracy.”

Accuracy rewards doing nothing

About 1–2% of buildings fail in a given spell. A model that predicts “no failure” for every building in New York scores over 98% accuracy while being completely useless. With rare events, accuracy is the wrong yardstick — so we don't report it, and you shouldn't trust anyone who does.

Precision at depth is the real question

The city has limited inspectors. “90 at the top 100” means: of the first 100 buildings our list says to visit, about 90 genuinely lose heat during the spell — the hit rate of roughly the first day of inspections. And we never show one cutoff alone: every evaluation reports the top 100, 500, 1,000 and 2,500, so no single flattering number can carry the story. The ranking holds up beyond the top too: the top 1% of our list captures 26% of all heat failures in a spell, and the top 10% captures 75% — against 1% and 10% for random inspection.

Two different promises, both graded

The ranked list is graded by precision at depth (who to visit first). The percentages shown on building pages are graded by calibration — checked bin by bin, so “60%” means failure really happens about 60% of the time in that group. One model, two measured guarantees. That discipline has teeth: we rejected a model that won at the top 100 because it lost at the depths where the city actually inspects.

04 / Ranges & calibration

Uncertainty we measured and chose to show.

Why ranges

A single number can be lucky. Every headline carries a bootstrap confidence interval — the evaluation data is resampled thousands of times, including in blocks of consecutive days, because days inside one cold snap rise and fall together. The model's edge over the best baseline survives at p ≈ 0.00001 on daily rankings, and it won all 8 of 8 held-out cold spells.

Why capped and banded

Every probability on this site is calibrated, then capped at 99% (nothing is certain) and shown as a band above 20% — because “61.3%” implies a precision no forecast honestly has. The calibration table is one click away on every building page.

05 / Open source

The code is open. The data stays safe.

Published

The feature pipeline, model training, the evaluation protocol, the statistical test suite, portable JSON model files, and this website. Source available on request while the team finalizes the license.

Deliberately not published

Raw complaint extracts (get them from NYC Open Data directly), anything apartment-level, and operational secrets. The underlying data is public: NYC 311, HPD violations, PLUTO and weather archives.

Built with Python, XGBoost, scikit-learn, Snap ML, Flask and deck.gl — running on IBM LinuxONE (s390x) and Vercel. HeatAhead is an independent project, not NYC311 or an HPD service.
